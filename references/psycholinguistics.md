# Psycholinguistic evidence for contextual understanding

Use this reference when wording, pronouns, hedging, or changes in conversational style might clarify a consequential uncertainty. It supports PREPARE and DIGEST through testable interpretations of language in context. A word-category frequency describes a text; a population association does not identify the writer's enduring personality, authority, sincerity, or intentions.

## What the instruments measure

LIWC compares tokens with versioned dictionaries and usually reports the percentage of all words matching each category. Categories overlap and have hierarchical relationships. Function words include pronouns, articles, conjunctions, auxiliary verbs, prepositions and negations; they describe grammatical choices across many topics. Content categories capture subject matter such as affiliation or work. Neither kind automatically supplies the pragmatic meaning of a sentence. For example, a negative-emotion word can occur inside a denial, quotation, joke, or description of somebody else. Preserve the sentence and conversational turn alongside any count. [P1, P2]

LIWC-22's tentative category includes ordinary conditional and alternative constructions; its manual gives examples including *if*, *or*, *any* and *something*. Therefore a tentative-category match is not identical to a human judgment of uncertainty. Conversely, uncertainty can be expressed without familiar hedge words. Pronouns require referent resolution: *we* can mean a team, company, couple, generic public, or an exclusive subgroup. An interviewer asking for personal history naturally elicits *I*. A manager explaining a team's work naturally elicits *we*. These are competing explanations to examine, not facts about the speaker inferred from counts. [P1; contextual applications are design deductions]

The LIWC-22 summary measures, including Clout and Authenticity, are standardized composites based on prior research and comparison corpora; the manual identifies these summaries as nontransparent dimensions. A Clout percentile is not verified decision authority. An Authenticity score is not a truthfulness test. Do not recreate a commercial instrument by inventing weights for selected pronouns or renaming a model impression a LIWC score. A plain-language rubric can instead classify an explicit claim, such as whether somebody names an approver. [P1; application deduction]

## What the primary evidence establishes

| Evidence | Observed result and design | What transfers to relationship work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LIWC-22 development manual, Boyd et al. (2022), P1 | Norming corpus comprises 1,000 texts from each of 11 sources, initially approximately 100–10,000 words each; over 31 million words overall. The manual reports its authors' typical text-emotion versus self-report correlations around .15–.20, with a .05–.40 range. These are contextual, heterogeneous associations, not a universal Big Five effect size. | Category construction, counting reliability, norming and construct validity are separate questions. A reliable category count can have weak correspondence with a proposed psychological construct. Select a comparison corpus appropriate to the communication setting. |
| Yarkoni (2010), P3 | Retrieved PMC material contains a facet-correlation table, not the complete article narrative. Its self-discipline row includes tentative language r=-.18; emotionality includes feeling words r=.26; intellect includes first-person-singular r=-.21. These are illustrative facet associations in the source table, not predictive rules. | Different facets associate with overlapping word categories. A hedge does not identify one trait. Do not collapse a facet into its parent Big Five dimension or interpret r as individual probability. Full narrative, adjustment and selection details were not verified in this access. |
| Schwartz et al. (2013), P4 | Approximately 75,000 English-primary-language Facebook volunteers; at least 1,000 words/person, mean 4,129 words over 206 updates. Roughly 72,000 completed personality questionnaires. Age and gender were controlled in differential language analyses; a separate prediction evaluation held out 25% of participants. Open-vocabulary features improved prediction relative to LIWC categories. | Rich language can contain personality-related information. This establishes neither the validity of an LLM's short-excerpt impression nor transfer from public Facebook updates to private work conversations. High/low classification after excluding middle scorers cannot classify arbitrary people. |
| Kacewicz et al. (2014), P5 | Five settings: 41 four-person groups with assigned leadership; 56 task pairs; 50 get-to-know-you pairs; email from nine participants; 40 Iraqi military letters. Accessible abstract and appendix show overall high-versus-low-status effects: first-person singular d=-.85, first-person plural d=.49, second person d=.29. Tentative language d=-.22 overall, with study estimates -.29, -.47, .02, -.26, -.04; certainty d=.04 overall. | Relative position and task can covary with pronoun use. The appendix itself shows heterogeneity: first-person-plural d=-.02 in email, although positive overall. Relative interactional rank does not establish formal approval power, expertise, or stable dominance. Tentative and certainty categories are not interchangeable status indicators. |
| Dudău and Sava (2021), P6 | 1,753 TED talks with English, Dutch, Brazilian Portuguese and Romanian counterparts: 7,012 transcripts; 62 LIWC2015 categories. Category agreement differed across dictionaries. Several grammar categories, power, reward and informal language showed poor agreement; roughly half were moderate. Authors recommend within-language standardization for pooled analysis. | Matching category names or correlated scores does not imply equal measurement across languages. Translation and semi-formal TED delivery are specific contexts; this is not proof of cross-cultural trait equivalence or a recipe for standardizing one person's small sample. |
| Martínez-Huertas et al. (2022), P7 | 643 Spanish psychology undergraduates, 13% male, mean age 19.5; requested 500-word self-descriptions, mean 363 words. Stepwise regressions repeated across 1,000 random 70/30 splits. Training R=.42–.47 across traits; mean validation R=.06–.14. A further five-fold procedure showed a similar weak-validation pattern. | Apparent fit and plausible post-hoc stories can survive in-sample while prediction deteriorates on new writers. This result concerns these samples/features/models; it does not disprove every language-personality model. It does directly challenge unvalidated universal mappings from LIWC indicators to personality. |

The positive large-corpus results and the failed smaller-sample validation differ in language, task, sample, feature set and model. Retain both rather than choosing the result that matches an appealing interpretation. Cohen's d is a standardized contrast between groups; r describes association. Neither is an anchor on a person's trait scale or a calibrated probability that a particular interpretation is correct.

## PREPARE: turn a language observation into useful inquiry

1. **Choose the decision first.** Identify what understanding would change the next interaction: approval route, preferred level of detail, unresolved condition, personal versus collective responsibility, or whether a proposal fits. Do not run a free-floating personality analysis merely because text exists.
2. **Record the literal observation.** Keep attributed span, date, topic, interlocutor and channel. State “qualified the delivery date with two conditions,” rather than “low confidence.” If making a numerical description, include numerator, denominator, dictionary/version, unit of analysis and any excluded text. Without an actual instrument, describe wording qualitatively.
3. **Form competing contextual explanations.** For a hedge: incomplete information, a genuine dependency, politeness, avoidance of overpromising, domain convention, or a general speaking habit. For *we*: shared ownership, organizational voice, or an unclear referent. For reduced *I*: topic or prompt changes as well as any status hypothesis. Choose alternatives that lead to different practical actions.
4. **Check within-person comparability.** Compare separate occasions in the same language, medium, topic class and role when available; do not count duplicated notes as independent evidence. Ask whether the pattern changes with a particular dependency, audience or task. A stable habit is still not a validated trait estimate. A small personal baseline has no population-norm percentile.
5. **Choose one natural question or reflection.** Ask about the referent, condition or process that matters. Explain privately which answers would distinguish the hypotheses and what would change your guidance. If wording has no practical consequence, leave it uninterpreted and proceed with the substantive conversation.

| Observation and unresolved distinction | Possible move, adapted to the actual history | Answer branches and resulting action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “We should be able to do it” leaves ownership unclear. | “When you say ‘we,’ who would be involved in deciding and doing it?” | Named approver/implementer clarifies role; a generic team reference leaves authority unresolved. Map the process rather than raising a dominance score. |
| Tentative language surrounds one delivery date. | “Which part of that timing is still uncertain?” | A named dependency supports situational uncertainty; “that timing is firm; that's how I usually phrase it” corrects the hedge interpretation. Adjust planning or record the correction. |
| A proposal receives conditional assent. | Reflect the condition: “It sounds workable if the handoff is light enough. What would make that manageable?” | Concrete resource thresholds help assess fit; a new objection revises the working account. Neither response warrants a personality label. |
| Strong collective wording may conceal differing views. | “Does that describe everyone's view, or are there perspectives we haven't covered?” | Explicit disagreement suggests further consultation; explicit delegation clarifies whose account this is. An unanswered question leaves the distribution of views unknown. |
| More personal wording appears in a retrospective account. | Ask what changed in the work rather than why they used *I*. | A new responsibility can explain the wording. If no comparison is available, retain the observation without a longitudinal claim. |

These example moves are design proposals grounded in the measurement limits, not interventions proven by the cited studies to improve relationships or sales outcomes. Generate wording from the actual conversation; do not routinely interrogate pronouns.

## DIGEST: preserve meaning and revise the account

Read complete relevant turns before counting. Separate the person's speech from interviewer prompts, quotation, forwarded email, paraphrase, transcription uncertainty and template language. Resolve what *I*, *we*, *you* and *they* refer to where possible. Preserve negation, tense, conditionals and the difference between an opinion, intention, commitment and report of past conduct.

For each consequential observation, record:

- **Observed wording:** source span and context; optionally a reproducible rate, with its denominator and instrument.
- **Interpretations:** the leading situational explanation and plausible alternatives; no numerical confidence invented from a lexical effect size.
- **Discriminating answer or behavior:** what was actually said or done, including a correction or disconfirmation. A preparation question that was never asked supplies no evidence.
- **Revision:** which specific hypothesis strengthened, weakened, narrowed or stayed unresolved; whether the pattern applies only in this setting.
- **Practical implication:** the changed next step, such as clarifying a dependency, consulting a named approver, shortening a follow-up, or leaving the plan unchanged.
- **Next gap:** an optional question only if resolving it could materially change collaboration.

Example, wholly synthetic: “I think we can approve it after Finance checks the renewal date.” An appropriate digest records conditional approval language, unresolved identity of *we*, and a Finance dependency. It can hypothesize that timing is contingent on that check. It cannot establish that this speaker personally holds approval authority, is indecisive, or has low conscientiousness. If the next turn states “I own the budget; Finance only checks dates,” update the authority account from that explicit evidence. The pronoun pattern adds little once a direct statement resolves the question.

## Question and rubric fit

Use a textual rubric only when the target is observable in the supplied words. Useful targets include “explicit condition attached to agreement,” “named decision participant,” “personal versus attributed claim,” and “current versus historical process.” Give explicit anchors, separate insufficient evidence from a negative assertion, and select a grounding span. A condition's existence and whether it blocks action are different judgments.

A rubric for tentative *wording* must specify its unit: dictionary-token frequency, human-coded epistemic qualification, or conditional commitment. These constructs differ. A lexicon including *or* could count “Tuesday or Wednesday” as tentative, although both dates may be firm options. A semantic code can capture “I cannot commit until the test finishes” even without *maybe*. Do not train or evaluate the two as if they were the same outcome.

Big Five or authority labels require different evidence from textual-explicitness labels. If an explicit self-description is relevant, retain it as self-report with its date and context. Do not assign a trait score from pronoun counts or interpret a model's confidence as psychometric validity. For research that genuinely seeks personality prediction, predefine the outcome measure, language/domain, participant-level splits, feature selection inside training folds, untouched validation, uncertainty, subgroup checks and calibration. Validate against an appropriate independent measure; label agreement alone establishes neither construct validity nor practical benefit. These are methodological design requirements, not validation already performed by this skill.

## Length, modality, culture and calibration

- **Length:** LIWC's official guidance warns about texts below roughly 25–50 words and says longer texts produce more dependable results. That is a caution, not a universal minimum or guarantee at 50 words. Schwartz's 1,000-word inclusion floor and Yarkoni's long-blog design concern other tasks. Do not import those cutoffs as automatic licenses to infer a person. Short text can establish a direct commitment even when it cannot support stable rates.
- **Sampling:** Report how excerpts were selected. Selecting only conflict turns or one answer to a self-focused question changes pronoun and affect rates. Several turns from one meeting are dependent observations; many copied records are still one source event.
- **Modality:** Text supports lexical and semantic observations. A transcript omits or imperfectly captures timing, overlap, stress, intonation, laughter and hesitation. Punctuation inserted by transcription software is not an observed voice cue. Audio/video methods need their own actual evidence and validation; lexical results do not establish gaze, emotion, vocal confidence or body-language interpretations.
- **Language and culture:** Identify original language, translation, code switching and dictionary version. Distinguish cultural conventions from linguistic measurement differences; the four-language TED study cannot disentangle all of them. Avoid English pronoun baselines for a language that routinely omits subjects, and avoid culturally confident status readings from translated prose. Within-language standardization needs a suitable reference distribution; it is not a repair for one translated excerpt.
- **Individual calibration:** Begin with the person's explicit meanings and correction. Compare multiple relevant situations before calling a pattern consistent. Revisit after role, audience or circumstances change. Within-person change and between-person rank are different estimands: a population correlation does not establish that this person's greater hedge rate means greater uncertainty today. Calibration here means checking interpretations against subsequent evidence, not claiming a validated personal psychometric model.

Stop when the practical distinction is resolved, the person corrects the interpretation, evidence is too thin or noncomparable, or another method provides a more direct answer. Keep the useful uncertainty visible without constructing an elaborate language profile.
