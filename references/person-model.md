# Evolving person and relationship model

The central artifact is a useful, revisable explanation of this person in the contexts the user knows them. PREPARE and DIGEST are how that explanation learns. A meeting summary is an input; a preference list is only a small part of the model.

## Model dimensions

Develop the dimensions that matter to this relationship, rather than filling every field mechanically:

- **Values and priorities:** what the person says matters, what choices suggest matters, and where these differ or change.
- **Motivations:** desired outcomes, concerns, sources of satisfaction, and reasons the person gives for investing effort. Separate their explanation from ours.
- **Decision process:** criteria, evidence requirements, tradeoffs, dependencies, stakeholders, reversibility and timing.
- **Communication and working patterns:** how they seek clarity, handle uncertainty, disagree, coordinate or make commitments, scoped to observed contexts.
- **Tensions:** competing priorities, aspirations versus constraints, and differences across roles or situations.
- **Shared history:** experiences, promises, corrections, successes and friction that shape this particular relationship.
- **Consequential gaps:** what remains uncertain that could change understanding or the next interaction.

Do not infer stability merely from repeated words. A contextual pattern can be useful without becoming a global personality claim. A person's account of themselves, behavior in a particular meeting, and another person's interpretation are different evidence types.

## Build explanatory hypotheses

For each consequential hypothesis, capture:

1. A specific proposition and its scope: person, situation, period and relationship.
2. Supporting evidence with attributable spans and event dates.
3. A plausible alternative explanation and evidence against the leading account.
4. Qualitative confidence with reasons: directness, speaker reliability, independent occasions, context coverage and counterexamples.
5. What would distinguish the explanations and why that distinction matters.
6. The current implication for the user's next conversation or action.

Use [hypothesis card](../assets/hypothesis-card.md) when helpful. Confidence describes an evidence position, not a numerical probability of knowing someone's mind. Multiple model judgments over one answer do not increase the number of observations.

### Example: the same choice can mean different things

A collaborator declines a broad pilot and asks for one small test. Possible explanations include a preference for controlled experimentation, temporary delivery pressure, a need to protect customer experience, or doubt about this particular proposal. Record the actual request first. Compare prior situations before preferring one explanation. If there is no comparison, keep the alternatives active.

The useful next uncertainty might be whether a small test is a general decision strategy or a response to current capacity. That distinction changes which proposal and evidence would help. It is richer than assigning a label such as “risk averse.”

## PREPARE: design inquiry that could change the model

Recall existing answers before proposing questions. Select a small number of consequential gaps, not a quota. For each optional conversational move, privately give the user:

- **Learning aim:** the hypotheses or missing context it could distinguish.
- **Natural formation:** connect to actual shared history, the person's vocabulary and the upcoming purpose. Avoid premises that assume the hypothesis is true.
- **Interpretation branches:** what different answers could support, what they would leave unresolved, and what would count against the leading interpretation.
- **Responsive follow-up principle:** what to clarify if a particular distinction emerges; do not prewrite a mandatory sequence.
- **Practical implication:** how learning the distinction could change collaboration, a proposal or the next preparation.

Generate wording for the actual context. Method examples are illustrations of reasoning and must not become routine scripts. A reflection, concise explanation, relevant option or useful practical advice may reveal more than another question. Structure a conversation without scripting the person's responses.

In the pilot example, the user could revisit the collaborator's earlier request and explore what made a bounded test useful on that occasion. The wording should fit their actual history. A capacity explanation would support changing scope or timing; a general evidence preference would support a staged demonstration; an objection to the proposal would require examining its value. An answer may support several at once.

## DIGEST: learn from what actually happened

Keep preparation separate from the record of the conversation. An unasked suggested question supplies no evidence. Attribute speakers and distinguish transcript wording, notes, paraphrases and later recollections.

For each meaningful new observation:

1. Identify the claim, behavior or correction and its context.
2. Compare it with the recalled model and active alternatives.
3. Mark the hypothesis as supported, weakened, narrowed, overturned or unresolved; explain why.
4. Update the dimension and guidance that actually changed, preserving source lineage and contextual exceptions.
5. Identify the next consequential gap and carry it into the next PREPARE.

Explicit correction changes the working interpretation. Do not preserve a disconfirmed story by explaining away every counterexample. Conversely, one new incident need not erase a pattern across different contexts. Explain the relationship between observations.

### Longitudinal example

January notes suggest someone welcomes ambitious experiments. April conversation evidence says their team is at capacity and mistakes would now affect customers. DIGEST should weaken “novelty is the dominant current criterion,” distinguish enduring interest in exploration from present delivery priorities, and revise the next preparation toward bounded evidence, workload and customer effects. It should not merely append “busy in April” while continuing to recommend an expansive trial.

Event time and import time differ. A January note imported in May does not supersede April evidence simply because it arrived later. See [memory lifecycle](memory-lifecycle.md) for correction, temporal scope and projection updates.

## Depth and usefulness

Prefer an explanation that changes a useful decision over elaborate labels that change nothing. Review tensions and exceptions, not only confirming details. Ask what the current model predicts in this context, how it could be wrong, and what observation would change the next guidance. The result should become more discriminating over time: clearer about motivations and criteria, more sensitive to context, and better able to recognize when earlier assumptions no longer apply.
